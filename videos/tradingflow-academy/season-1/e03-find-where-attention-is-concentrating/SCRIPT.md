# SCRIPT — Find Where Attention Is Concentrating

Target runtime: 6:04  
Narration: English, HeyGen Marcus, calm professional analyst delivery  
Teaching surface: TradingFlow Rank · Symbols

## Scene 1 — The biggest print is not the answer · 0:00–0:28

The biggest premium print on a scanner is easy to notice. That does not make
it the most informative opportunity.

Fifty million dollars in a very large underlying and five million in a
smaller name are not equivalent observations.

Before you choose a contract, you need a better first question: where is
options activity concentrating relative to the name and the selected session?

That is the job of TradingFlow Rank · Symbols.

## Scene 2 — Why absolute leaderboards mislead · 0:28–1:10

An absolute leaderboard rewards scale.

The most liquid indices and mega-cap stocks naturally produce large premium,
high trade counts, and many active contracts. A smaller underlying can show a
much stronger relative impact while never reaching the top of a raw-dollar
list.

The opposite mistake is chasing one extreme normalized value without checking
liquidity, breadth, or freshness.

So do not ask, “Which number is biggest?” Ask, “Which names stand out on the
same metric, inside the same universe, for the same session?”

Ranking is a comparison framework, not a conclusion.

## Scene 3 — What Rank · Symbols actually ranks · 1:10–1:58

Rank · Symbols begins with its own complete canonical symbol universe.

It is not created by grouping whichever Contract Rank rows survived a filter.
That distinction matters. A traded-contract slice can omit quiet contracts
that still carry open interest, gamma, and volatility structure.

The Symbols view combines a full-chain structural snapshot with a separately
timestamped, lightweight flow overlay.

The structure answers questions about the option chain. The flow overlay
describes activity observed in the selected session.

They can appear in the same row, but they do not share one clock. TradingFlow
keeps their provenance separate so a fresh flow update is never mistaken for
a rebuilt structural snapshot.

## Scene 4 — Scope the session before you sort · 1:58–2:50

Start at the session label.

“Latest” means the most recent completed or active trading session. Before the
market opens, on a weekend, or on a holiday, that may be the prior completed
session rather than the current calendar date.

Historical mode freezes a chosen past session. It does not keep updating.

Next, confirm the universe and scope. Are you looking across the default
catalog, one underlying type, a watchlist, or a narrower cohort?

Rank order only has meaning inside that defined comparison set.

If the session, source, or freshness is unavailable, treat the rank as
incomplete evidence. Do not silently replace it with a different date or a
wider universe.

## Scene 5 — Separate magnitude from direction · 2:50–3:42

Now choose the ranking question.

Net DEI is useful for cross-name comparison because it measures the magnitude
of directional flow relative to the name’s average stock volume. In Rank,
Net DEI is always read as a non-negative magnitude.

Direction lives somewhere else.

Net DEX is the signed direction of today’s classified trade-tape flow.
Positive means the session leaned bullish by the product’s tape
classification; negative means bearish. Sentiment summarizes that sign.

Net DEX is not dealer inventory, a support or resistance level, or proof that
someone must hedge at a strike.

Use Total Premium, trade count, and traded-contract count as supporting
context. No single column carries the whole thesis.

## Scene 6 — Build a shortlist in Rank · Symbols · 3:42–4:32

Open Rank · Symbols.

First, lock the session. Second, choose one sorter. A practical discovery
starting point is Net DEI descending because it compares relative impact
across names of very different size.

Read the first few rows, not only rank one.

For each name, pair magnitude with direction. Then check total premium, trade
count, the number of traded contracts, underlying type, and the row’s
structure and flow provenance.

A smaller raw premium can deserve attention when its relative impact is
unusual. A huge premium can deserve less attention when it is normal for that
underlying.

The output is a shortlist of candidates, not a buy list.

## Scene 7 — Inspect the candidate, not the rank number · 4:32–5:07

Open a candidate’s symbol drawer.

Use Overview to orient, Positioning and Chain for structural context, Gamma
Exposure and Volatility for their specific questions, and the consent-gated
Flow view when you need deeper session evidence.

A candidate becomes more interesting when relative magnitude stands out,
direction is coherent, activity is broad enough to investigate, and every
source is fresh enough for the question.

Mixed direction, one isolated row, stale provenance, or thin activity does
not invalidate the candidate. It tells you what to verify next.

## Scene 8 — Practice the discovery method · 5:07–5:50

In Rank · Symbols, choose one completed session and one ranking metric.

Record three candidates. For each one, write the session, universe, sorter,
magnitude, direction, breadth, provenance, and the next question you need to
answer.

Do not write “bullish” or “bearish” as the final decision. Write why the name
earned inspection.

The rule is simple: rank tells you where to look. It does not tell you what
happens next.

In the next lesson, we will move from one ranked symbol to a specific contract
using expiry, strike, moneyness, repetition, and volume-to-open-interest.
